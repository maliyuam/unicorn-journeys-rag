What this is

This system reconstructs the entrepreneurial journeys of African unicorn founders from scattered public media — YouTube interviews, keynotes, panels and podcasts — and turns them into structured, source-cited narratives.

It is a rebuild of the method in “Generative AI Methodology for Uncovering Entrepreneurial Journeys in Africa” (Esibe, Aliyu, Thornburg, Barros & Stam), upgraded at every stage and wrapped in this interface. The research problem it addresses: data on African entrepreneurship is fragmented and largely unstructured, so the evidence exists but cannot be studied at scale by hand.

The pipeline

1
Discover — Search YouTube and podcast RSS for a founder's appearances. Non-API routes are tried first (yt-dlp keyless search, then Invidious mirrors); the official YouTube Data API is an optional booster, never a requirement.
2
Filter for relevance — Every candidate is judged against the founder: is it really them speaking or being discussed, or a name collision? Claude does this with a structured verdict and a stated reason; without credentials a keyword heuristic stands in.
3
You approve — Nothing is fetched until a human says so. Candidates arrive pre-checked with their relevance reasons; you uncheck what does not belong. A full-roster sweep automates the approval using the same filter, and everything it collects stays reviewable and deletable in the sources table.
4
Fetch transcripts — Five keyless strategies in order: the transcript API, yt-dlp captions via web then Android/iOS players, Invidious, Piped, and finally local Whisper ASR that reads the audio directly. Each item succeeds or fails on its own and reports which route worked.
5
Chunk and embed — Transcripts are split on sentence boundaries at about 220 words with overlap, with a hard word cap so unpunctuated auto-captions cannot collapse into one giant block. Each chunk is embedded with BGE-small (384-d) and tagged with founder, company, source and type.
6
Store — Chunks and vectors go to MongoDB Atlas, which serves retrieval through native $vectorSearch with a founder pre-filter. If the cluster is unreachable the app falls back to a local index rather than losing work, and syncs back up afterwards.
7
Retrieve — A question is expanded into several atomic sub-queries. Each runs through both semantic (vector) and keyword (BM25) search, and the rankings are merged with reciprocal-rank fusion. The hybrid matters: exact names, dates and dollar figures are precisely what dense vectors blur. A cross-encoder then re-reads the top candidates alongside the question and its ranking is blended with fusion's, which promotes passages that actually answer the question instead of ones that merely share its topic.
8
Generate — Claude writes the answer or the six-part founder narrative from the retrieved passages only, citing each statement inline as [S1]. The prompts state explicitly that the sources are machine transcripts with garbled names, no punctuation and unlabelled speakers.
9
Judge and refine — Each narrative is audited claim by claim for faithfulness and hallucination rate, and scored for Context Completeness across the six journey themes. If the hallucination rate crosses the threshold the narrative is rewritten with the unsupported claims flagged, re-judged, and only kept if it actually improved.

How we know it works

Aggregate scores hide bugs, so the Evaluate tab scores the live pipeline against a golden set of questions whose answers were read out of the ingested transcripts. Relevance is defined by evidence text appearing in a retrieved chunk, which keeps the labels valid when chunking or the embedding model changes.

Retrieval and generation are scored separately, so a wrong answer can be blamed on the stage that caused it. Every run saves a baseline and the next run reports the delta. This is what caught a chunking bug that had flattened whole interviews into single chunks, and it is what rejected a retrieval change that looked sensible but measurably made things worse.

What it does not do

  • It does not know anything beyond the transcripts. If the sources never mention a funding round, the narrative says so rather than filling the gap from memory.
  • Coverage follows media, not merit. A founder who gives few interviews gets a thin narrative. Low completeness reflects what was documented publicly, not the founder.
  • Speech recognition makes mistakes. Names and figures can be garbled, and panel recordings do not label who is speaking, so some attributions carry real uncertainty.

Upload transcript files

Accepts .txt, .md, .json, .srt, .vtt (YouTube caption exports are cleaned automatically). JSON files may embed their own founder/company/source_title; the fields below are defaults for files that don't. Re-uploading an unchanged file is skipped; a changed file replaces its previous chunks.

Attribution audit

Has the LLM read each stored transcript and judge whether the founder it was filed under actually appears in it. This is the check that string matching cannot do: the corpus contains “Tosi and La” for Tosin Eniolorunda and “Helen” for Halan, and companies rename (Moniepoint was TeamApt). Filing one person's interview under another founder produces citations that look perfectly legitimate.
